Part 1: Stories we reason with

We reason with narratives, analogies and metaphors all the time: in daily life, in teaching and in research. The workshop calls them NAMs for short. A NAM lets you predict what comes next, and every NAM breaks somewhere.

Six NAMs: what each lets you predict, and where it breaks
WhereThe NAMLets you predictBreaks when
Daily life“Traffic is flowing.” (metaphor)A jam spreads backward, like a wave in a pipe.Drivers make choices; water doesn’t.
Daily life“My phone is dying.” (metaphor)It needs charging soon, or it stops.A dead phone comes back once you plug it in.
Teaching“An atom is a tiny solar system.” (analogy)Electrons circle the nucleus like planets.Electrons don’t follow orbits, so it fails for chemistry.
Teaching“Memory is a filing cabinet.” (metaphor)Memories are stored, then pulled out intact.Remembering rebuilds a memory, and can change it.
Research“Natural selection” (analogy with a breeder’s choices)Helpful traits spread over generations.No one is doing the selecting.
Research“DNA is a blueprint.” (metaphor)The genes spell out the finished body.Development depends on the environment too.

A story picks what comes next

An everyday case: a colleague hasn’t answered your email in a week. Those are the facts. The story you tell about them decides what you do.

Three stories about the same unanswered email
The storyWhat you expect nextWhat you do
“She’s swamped.”A reply, eventually.Wait. Maybe a gentle nudge.
“She’s annoyed with me.”Silence, or a cool reply.Apologize. Walk over to her office.
“It went to spam.”Nothing, until you fix it.Resend from another account.

The point: same facts, a different story, a different next step. The NARC lab built puzzles to study this, in people and in AI models.

Part 2: How a NARC puzzle works, and how the models did

NARC stands for Narrative Augmented Reasoning Challenges. Each puzzle has three parts.

  1. A strip of small grids. Three to eight frames that show something happening. One frame is hidden.
  2. A short story. It doesn’t describe the grids. It helps only if you read it as being about the shapes.
  3. Rebuild the hidden grid. Exactly, every cell the right color. Close doesn’t count.

There are about 320 public puzzles at narc.insight.uidaho.edu. Anyone can solve them, and anyone can submit one.

Try one: Chat

The grids, in plain words. Each grid is 3 cells by 3 cells. A gray bar stands in the left column and an orange bar in the right column. Both grow up from the bottom. The middle column is always empty.

  1. Grid 1: gray bar 2 cells tall, orange bar 2 cells tall.
  2. Grid 2: gray bar drops to 1 cell; orange stays at 2.
  3. Grid 3: gray bar rises to 3 cells, the full height; orange stays at 2.
  4. Grid 4: hidden. What goes here?

The story

Ally and Bob met to chat. Ally became sad. After some encouraging words from Bob, Ally became happy. This made Bob happy too.

Reading it: gray is Ally, orange is Bob, and the height of a bar is mood. Ally drops when she’s sad and comes back higher when she’s happy. The answer: “This made Bob happy too,” so in grid 4 both bars are 3 cells tall.

How to read a results table

Every puzzle is also given to AI models. The students’ puzzles in Part 3 use the same kind of table, so here is how to read one.

  1. Each row is one AI model.
  2. Three tests. Grids only: the model sees the strip and fills in the hidden grid. Story only: it gets the story and is told how many grids there are and which one is hidden. Both: grids and story together. “Yes” means it got every cell right.
  3. Two follow-ups, run three times each, only for models that solved it with both. Shuffled grids: the same grids and story, with the grids in a random order; the number is how many of 3 shuffles the model still solved. Key words only: the story replaced by an alphabetized list of its key words; the number is how many of 3 tries it solved. For Chat, the key words are: ally, became, bob, chat, encouraging, happy, made, met, sad, words.
  4. Narrate’s model, qwen3.8-27b, is the one participants write for in Part 4.

The NARC property: a puzzle has it for a model when the model needs both the grids and the story. The story did work the pictures couldn’t do alone. One caution: each model gets one try per test, and reruns change about one verdict in six, so read the pattern across models rather than any single result.

What we mean by a model, and where they run

  • A model is one trained AI system. Chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude are apps built on models. Each row in the tables is a different model.
  • Different makers, different sizes. Google (gemma), OpenAI (gpt-oss), Alibaba (qwen), Moonshot AI (kimi), NVIDIA (nemotron). “Open weights” means the maker released the model itself, so anyone can run it. The number in a name is roughly its size in billions of parameters, the numbers a model learns in training: 120b means 120 billion.
  • Most run on MindRouter. MindRouter is the University of Idaho’s own AI service. According to the MindRouter site, every model it serves runs on university-owned GPUs, and prompts and replies stay on university infrastructure. Eleven of the twelve models in the students’ tables are open models that ran on it. Two of those (glm-5.3-flash and mimo-v2.6-flash) were retired from MindRouter on October 1, 2026. Claude Fable 5.1 is a commercial model, run off campus, for comparison.
  • Open to you. Sign in with your university account at mindrouter.uidaho.edu. You can chat in the browser, or create a key to use it from your own code. Narrate uses it too.

Part 3: Three puzzles from the NARC lab

Three undergraduates in the lab each made a puzzle, tested it against the models and wrote down what they learned. The result tables below are copied from the public results page on October 21, 2026, and read the same way as the Chat table in Part 2. They add a twelfth model, Claude Fable 5.1, the commercial one.

Hunt, by Hadley

The grids, in plain words. Each grid is 5 by 5. Rows are numbered from the top and columns from the left. Green cells (grass) sit at row 1 column 4, row 4 column 4, and row 5 column 2 in every grid.

  1. Grid 1: four yellow cells (ducks) at row 2 column 2, row 3 column 4, row 5 column 1, and row 5 column 5.
  2. Grid 2: the duck at row 2 column 2 has turned red. The ducks at row 3 column 4 and row 5 column 1 are gone. The duck at row 5 column 5 is still yellow.
  3. Grid 3: the duck at row 5 column 5 has turned red too.
  4. Grid 4: hidden.

Hadley’s story

Treb likes to go hunting in his free time. In the marshy area behind his house, a flock of ducks likes to hang out. The green grass can sometimes make shooting the ducks difficult, as the ducks will hide behind them. Treb was lucky enough to snipe one duck. However, the shot startled the surrounding ducks, and the ones closest to the grass hid. Treb noticed one duck still sitting out. After getting two ducks, he collects his stuff and leaves. The remaining ducks emerge from the grass to discover only bloody puddles left of their friends.

The answer: the two ducks that hid come back out, yellow again at row 3 column 4 and row 5 column 1. The two red cells stay where they are, and the grass doesn’t change.

Reading the story

Hadley’s identifiers for human readers: ducks are yellow, grass is green, blood is red, hiding means not visible, and emerging means visible again. Hadley also wrote a simplified version that names the colors:

Simplified story

The yellow ducks like to sit in the green grass. The hunter comes along and shoots two yellow ducks. The other two ducks hide behind the green grass. When the two yellow ducks emerge from behind the green grass, they see the red puddles left of their friends.

What Hadley learned

  • The hunter is never in the picture. Treb never appears in the grids, but he decides which ducks are there. An agent can be present in the story and absent from the scene.
  • Guess, then intuitive, then obvious. Without the story you guess. With it, the answer feels obvious.
  • Simple and complex stories both matter. The same solution can be told in ways that look very different.
  • One problem, many perspectives. Different readers, one right answer. Next: one problem, many stories, which is what Narrate is for.

The Accident, by Jason

The grids, in plain words. Each grid is 6 by 6, rows numbered from the top.

  1. Grid 1: light blue fills the top five rows, except a 2-by-2 yellow block in the top right corner. A red cell sits at row 5 column 2. The bottom row is black.
  2. Grid 2: everything is black except a 2-by-2 gray block in the top left corner and the red cell, still at row 5 column 2.
  3. Grid 3: the gray block has moved to the top middle (columns 3 and 4). The red cell is still at row 5 column 2. A green cell has appeared at row 5 column 4.
  4. Grid 4: hidden.

Jason’s story

A car stalls out on an empty highway in the middle of the desert. The driver has no cellphone service. All they can do is wait and hope that someone passes by to help. In the middle of the night, a giant tortoise approaches the vehicle. For hours, it sits and stares, wondering about the strange, still beast before it. Suddenly, the driver, who has fallen asleep at this point, feels a jolt as the car loses traction and crushes the curious tortoise.

The answer: the gray block has moved on to the top right corner (columns 5 and 6). The red cell has moved to row 5 column 4, where the green cell was, and the green cell is gone.

Reading the story

Jason’s identifiers for human readers: the sun is yellow, the sky is blue and then black, the car is red, the tortoise is green, and the moon is gray. Neither version of the story mentions the sun or the moon; the reader supplies them. Jason also wrote a simplified version that names the colors:

Simplified story

A red car stalls out on an empty highway. In the middle of the night, a giant green tortoise approaches the red vehicle. The driver, who has fallen asleep at this point, feels a jolt as the car loses traction and crushes the curious tortoise.

  • Anyone can play, make or judge these puzzles, and learn how AI thinks while they do it. The puzzles invite teaching, learning and work across fields.

Ballerina 4, by Chloe

The grids, in plain words. Each grid is 5 by 5, rows numbered from the top. Light blue cells are dancers in the crew; one red cell is the ballerina.

  1. Grid 1: the crew forms a V in the top three rows (row 1 columns 1 and 5, row 2 columns 2 and 4, row 3 column 3). The ballerina is in the bottom left corner.
  2. Grid 2: the crew forms an upside-down V (row 1 column 3, row 2 columns 2 and 4, row 3 columns 1 and 5). The ballerina is still in the bottom left corner.
  3. Grid 3: crew at row 1 columns 1 and 5, row 3 columns 1 and 5, and row 4 column 3. The ballerina has moved to row 4 column 2.
  4. Grid 4: hidden.
  5. Grid 5: the whole crew stands in a line across the top row. The ballerina is gone.

Chloe’s story

A ballerina collaborated with a street dance crew for a performance. The street dance crew took the stage first, constantly changing formations and movements in sync with the music. The ballerina waited at the back of the stage for her entrance. As the music gradually slowed down, she began to join the performance. When the performance reached its climax, only the ballerina performed at the center of the stage while all the street dance crew members paused at the back of the stage. At the end of the show, the ballerina fully exited, and the street dance crew finished the performance at the very front of the stage in an unchanged formation.

The answer: the ballerina alone at the center (row 3 column 3), and the whole crew in a line across the bottom row.

Reading the story

Red is the ballerina and light blue is the street dance crew. The top row is the front of the stage and the bottom row is the back. Solving it takes both understanding the story and tracking where every dancer stands from one frame to the next.

Part 4: Your turn, on Narrate and MindRouter

So far the stories were given. In Narrate you write them, and you see the answer that the model doesn’t.

How Narrate works

  1. You see every grid, including the one the model won’t see.
  2. You write a story about what happens. Don’t spell out cells, numbers or coordinates.
  3. The model gets the other grids and your story, and has to rebuild the hidden one exactly. The model is qwen3.8-27b, running on MindRouter.
  4. You see whether it worked. Revise and try again.

The activity: write a different story (12 minutes)

  1. Go to narc.insight.uidaho.edu/narrate and enter the invite code AISYMP2026 (valid through November 30, 2026).
  2. Pick a puzzle. Chat is the quickest.
  3. Write two to five sentences. A new setting is welcome: cooking, sports, your own field.
  4. Submit. A test takes up to two minutes. If the page says the model is busy, wait a minute and try again.
  5. Read the result, revise, and try again.

Challenge: Narrate’s model failed The Accident with Jason’s story. Can yours get it there?

Research notice: Narrate is a research study for adults 18 or older, and it’s voluntary. We don’t ask for your name or email. Stories may be published without your name, so leave personal information out.

No laptop, or the site is down? Write your story on a card. Bert will read a few aloud and test them from the front.

Part 5: Stories about AI that leave us room to choose

In Part 1, the story you tell about an unanswered email picked your next step. The stories we tell about AI do the same thing for us. Some leave us something to decide. Some leave us nothing.

A new AI tool arrives in your department

Three stories about the same news
The storyWhat you expect nextWhat you doWho decides
“It’s a wave.”It arrives whatever we do.Adapt fast, or fall behind.No one. It’s weather.
“It’s an arms race.”Whoever slows down loses.Adopt it everywhere, now.Rivals, somewhere else.
“It’s a forklift.”It lifts whatever you point it at.Decide what’s worth lifting yourself.You.

The point: same news. The first two stories leave us nothing to decide. The third hands the decision back.

Two stories from the summer Faculty AI Bootcamp: the gym and the treadmill

  • Nobody brings a forklift to the gym. The weight is the point. Some friction in our work is the reps, the part that builds the skill: drafting your own argument, working a proof, sitting with a hard reading. Some is the commute: formatting citations, converting files. Hand off the commute. Keep the reps. The line moves: grammar is commute in a lab report and reps in a writing seminar.
  • The productivity treadmill. AI saves an hour, and the hour fills with more output. We run faster and stay in the same place.
  • Add a plate on purpose. Some frictions we choose: try it before you ask, have the AI quiz you instead of answer you, check one claim against the source.

Both stories ask the same two questions. Which friction is the point? And where does the saved time go?

Where should AI go? Keep it human, use it with friction, or hand it off

Stories that look ahead: it’s 2031, and AI went well here. How?

Most stories about AI’s future are things that happen to us. These four are stories where people chose. Each asks something of us now.

Four stories about the future, and what each asks of us now
The storyLooking back from 2031What it asks of us now
Our own toolsWe ran our own AI and set its rules together, the way we run a library.Show up when we decide what it’s for.
The sparring partnerAI made students do more reps, not fewer. It argued back and quizzed them.Set it up to question, not to answer.
Off the treadmillThe hours AI saved went to people: office hours, fieldwork, slow reading.Decide where saved time goes before it’s saved.
The second opinionAI was never the first word or the last. People formed a view, then asked.Think first, ask second, and make the call yourself.

A test for any story about AI’s future: does it say who decides, what we keep doing ourselves, and where the saved time goes?

Table discussion (6 minutes)

  1. Who decides? Pick a story about AI you hear in your unit. Who acts in it? What does it leave you to decide?
  2. Keep, add friction, hand off. Name one task in your work for each. Where would you add a plate on purpose?
  3. Tell it from 2031. AI went well here. In two sentences, what did we choose?

Each table then reports one line: its story from 2031, something it would keep human, and a friction it will choose.

To take with you, and links

Choosing the story is a judgment call

  • Stories fill gaps. For people and for models, a story settles what the facts leave open.
  • Many true stories, some work. You can’t tell which by reading. You try it and check it against what you know.
  • Tell stories that leave you choices. Ones that say who decides, which frictions we keep, and where the saved time goes.
